IBALONG

Bicol Folk Epic

A 60-stanza fragment of the foundational Bicolano oral epic — recounting the heroic ages of Baltog, Handyong, and Bantong as they civilize the ancient land of Ibalong against mythical monsters, floods, and volcanic catastrophe.

Region: Bicol, Southern Luzon Era: Pre-Colonial Transcribed: 19th Century Language: Spanish (originally Bicolano) Stanzas: 60 (surviving fragment)

Three Narrative Pillars

The Hero Baltog

The first hero of Ibalong, who clears the land, kills the giant boar Tandayag, and introduces agriculture.

Handyong & Civilization

Defeats a multitude of wild beasts, creates laws, invents tools, and civilizes Ibalong with the aid of the shapeshifting serpent Oryol.

Bantong & Rabot

The third hero, who slays the petrifying half-man, half-beast monster Rabot, restoring safety and peace to the realm.

Scholarly Analysis

Research Documentation

The Ibalong Epic — Academic Research Paper

✦

1. What Is It?

The Ibalong (also spelled Ibálong) is a 60-stanza fragment of a longer Bicolano folk epic, considered the foundational literary and oral-tradition artwork of the Bicol region of the Philippines. As a folk epic — a genre also represented elsewhere in the archipelago by the Ilocano Biag ni Lam-ang, the Maranao Darangen, and the Panay Hinilawod — the Ibalong belongs to a class of pre-colonial narrative poetry that recounts, in heroic and often supernatural terms, the founding, settlement, and civilizing of a people's ancestral land.

Structurally, the surviving text is a narrative verse poem told through the frame device of a bard, Kadunung, who is asked by a listener named Iling to sing of the “glorious Ibálong of long ago.” What survives today is not the complete epic; the fragment ends abruptly mid-story, with the bard promising to continue “some other time” — a continuation that was apparently never recorded.

The epic is often compared to a founding or origin myth, chronicling three successive warrior-heroes — Baltog, Handyong, and Bantong — who each rid the ancient land of monstrous beings, alongside a “golden age” of cultural inventions (agriculture, tools, boats, weaving, pottery, and writing) attributed to named culture-heroes. Scholars have also noted structural and thematic parallels to the Indian Ramayana and Mahabharata, given the epic's references to heroes originating from “Boltavara” (believed to reference Bharata-varsha, an old name for India).

Today, the Ibalong functions simultaneously as: (a) a literary text studied in Philippine literature curricula, (b) a historical-anthropological source for pre-colonial Bicolano life, and (c) a living cultural tradition, re-enacted annually in festival, theater, and visual-art form.

2. Where Is It From?

The epic is rooted in the Bicol Region (Region V) of southeastern Luzon, Philippines, and specifically narrates events set in the ancient territory called “Ibalong” or “Aslon.” Historically, the entire Bicol peninsula was referred to as Ibalong, until it was administratively divided in 1636.

Key locations in or associated with the epic:

  • Tondol (Camalig, Albay) — where Baltog settled and planted his crops
  • Ligmanan / Libmanan (Camarines Sur) — claimed seat of the ancient “Kingdom of Ibalong”
  • Mount Isarog, Mount Masaraga, Mount Asog, Mount Hantik — volcanic landmarks tied to epic events
  • Lake Bato and Lake Buhi — bodies of water said to have formed during the epic’s cataclysmic flood
  • Pasacao (Camarines Sur) — seacoast said to have emerged from the sea during the disaster
  • The Bicol River — site of Handyong’s battles against crocodiles, said to have run red with their blood

Geographically, the epic’s world roughly corresponds to what is today the provinces of Albay and Camarines Sur, with Legazpi City (Albay) now serving as the epic’s primary modern festival home.

3. Who Is Involved?

Figures Within the Narrative

  • Kadunung — the legendary native bard credited with originally composing and reciting the epic
  • Iling — the listener who requests the tale at the poem’s opening

Historical Transmitters & Scholars

  • Fray Bernardino de Melendreras de la Trinidad, OFM (1815–1867) — Franciscan friar who first collected and transcribed the narrative into a Spanish-language manuscript (since lost)
  • Fray Jose Castaño, OFM — published a 60-stanza excerpt in 1895
  • Luis G. Dato — Bicolano poet laureate who translated the fragment into English verse in 1965
  • Merito B. Espinas — scholar whose full English translation appeared in the Far Eastern University Faculty Journal in 1996
  • Ma. Lilia F. Realubit — Bicolano literary scholar whose analysis of the epic’s ending is widely cited
  • Zeus Salazar — Filipino historian who wrote on the etymology of “Ibalon”

Modern Creative Custodians

  • Abdon M. Balde Jr. — author of Awit ni Kadunung (2008), a 623-page Filipino novelistic expansion of the epic
  • Rody Vera and Tuxqs Rutaquio — playwright and director of Ibalong: The Musical (Tanghalang Pilipino)
  • Community organizers of the Ibalong Festival in Legazpi City

4. What Are Its Characteristics?

Literary Style & Structure

The Ibalong survives as a 60-stanza narrative poem in Spanish, written in a ballad-like rhyming quatrain structure, framed as an oral performance delivered by a bard to a listening audience. It follows an episodic, cumulative structure: each of the three heroes confronts and overcomes a distinct set of obstacles, escalating in scale. Notably, the epic ends without resolution or moral closure — the bard simply promises a continuation that history has not preserved, giving the text an unusually open and melancholic tone.

Oral Tradition & Transmission

Like most Philippine folk epics, the Ibalong was originally an orally transmitted chant, performed rather than read, and only survives because it was transcribed by Spanish friars in the 19th century. No complete pre-colonial Bicolano-language version survives; the fragment’s oldest extant text is in Spanish, not Bicol.

Core Themes

  • Bravery and heroism in the face of overwhelming, monstrous adversity
  • Wisdom and strategy over brute force (Bantong’s patience vs. Rabot)
  • Communal progress and civilization-building through named inventions
  • Resilience against natural disaster (flood, volcanic eruption, earthquake)
  • Social equity within hierarchy — Handyong’s laws protected both “slaves and masters”

5. What Is Its Cultural Significance?

The Ibalong holds a place in Bicolano culture comparable to that of a founding national epic — frequently described as an “invaluable piece of literature” recording the early struggles of the ancient Bicolanos for survival, order, and growth.

  • Proof of a pre-colonial civilization — demonstrates organized, law-governed, technologically inventive societies existed in Bicol before 1521
  • A named, mapped mythology — tied to real, still-existing places: Mount Isarog, Lake Bato, the Bicol River
  • A secular, non-religious festival tradition — the Ibalong Festival (Legazpi City, est. 1992) is explicitly civic and pre-colonial in its symbolism
  • Communal pride amid environmental hardship — the epic's disaster sequence mirrors Bicol's chronic vulnerability to typhoons and volcanic activity
  • A living, reinterpreted tradition — continues to generate serious literary and historical output

6. What Does It Tell Us About Bikolano Identity?

  • Relationship to land and water — the name “Ibalon” connects Bicolanos to river- and coast-dwelling identity
  • Courage paired with strategy — Bantong’s victory through patience, Handyong’s through alliance over domination
  • Orientation toward collective progress — distributes credit widely across named inventors for farming tools, weaving, pottery, and writing
  • Negotiated relationship with outside influence — all three heroes are foreign arrivals who civilize the land, an early acknowledgment of migration and exchange
  • Cultural memory of vulnerability and resilience — the cataclysm sequence is an inherited, almost geological memory of a hazard-prone homeland
  • Ambivalent relationship with history — Handyong mourning Rabot’s death has been read as foreboding the coming subjugation of the Bicolanos under Spanish rule

7. Why Should It Be Preserved?

  • It is one of a shrinking number of surviving pre-colonial Philippine epics — only a 60-stanza fragment remains of a once-complete work
  • It is a primary source for understanding pre-colonial Philippine society — agriculture, technology, law, and cosmology
  • It anchors a living regional identity — sustaining civic, artistic, and touristic life in Bicol
  • It resists a colonial-centered narrative of Philippine history by demonstrating organized civilization before foreign contact
  • Its documentary trail remains fragile — a 2024 discovery of a Melendreras-signed document in Libmanan was found deteriorating and termite-damaged
  • It offers enduring, transferable values — courage, wisdom, resilience — relevant for younger generations
